// part 02 · presenter view

A presenter view needs four pieces of information

①
Big current-slide preview

More than half the view. You should be able to scan it and instantly know what the audience sees.

②
Next-slide preview

Lets you queue up the transition sentence in advance — no "what was on the next slide again?".

③
Speaker-script panel

Big type, high contrast, scrollable. This is what the speaker is actually reading.

④
Timer + page number

Knowing how long you've been talking and how many slides remain — that's where pacing comes from.

The presenter view should give you four pieces of information. I'll rank them by importance.

First, the big current-slide preview. It has to take up more than half the window — it's your "sync anchor" with the audience. Whatever they see, you need to see in your head too.

Second, the next-slide preview. People often miss why this matters: the worst stall in a talk isn't on a slide, it's in the 2 seconds after a transition. If you can already see the next slide, the bridge sentence shows up on its own.

Third, the speaker-script panel — the focus of part three. The hard requirement here: the type must be big, the contrast must be high, the panel must scroll. Because while you're talking, you're only glancing — small text won't make it.

Fourth, the timer and page number. How long you've been going, how many slides you have left — pace lives here. Keynote nails this; reveal.js's defaults aren't loud enough.

All four, no skipping. This deck has all four — press S any time and try it out.

// part 03 · script

Three iron rules for the speaker script

01
It's not a verbatim script — it's "prompt signals"

Bold the core points, break out transition sentences, list data and names — so a single glance picks up the thread.

02
150–300 words per slide. Not more, not less.

Under 150 words you'll stall; over 300 you can't scan in time. Tune to a 2–3 min/slide rhythm.

03
Write it in spoken voice, not written prose

"Therefore" → "so". "The aforementioned approach" → "this approach". Read it out loud — if it doesn't sound like talking, rewrite.

Onto the most important part — how to write the speaker script. Three iron rules.

Rule 1: a speaker script isn't a script to read. Lots of folks hear "speaker script" and start writing every line out word-for-word. Wrong. If you read from a script, the audience spots it instantly and the trust drops to zero.

The real job of the script is "prompt signals" — bold the key points, break out transitions into their own lines, list out data and proper nouns. That way, you glance and pick up the thread, but what comes out is still your own voice.

Rule 2: 150–300 words per slide. This number comes from running a dozen-plus talks. Under 150, the prompts run out and you stall mid-paragraph. Over 300, your eye can't scan it in time. At 2–3 minutes per slide, this length lines up perfectly.

Rule 3: write it in spoken voice. This is the one most people fail at. If you write "therefore", you'll say "so". If you write "the aforementioned approach", you'll say "this approach". Read it out loud — if it doesn't sound natural, rewrite.

Combine the three and presenting suddenly stops being painful.
